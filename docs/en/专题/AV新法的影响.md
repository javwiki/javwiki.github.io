# Effects of Japan’s AV performer protection law

Japan's AV performer protection and relief law, commonly called the “AV new law,” took effect on **23 June 2022**. It changed contracting, filming, and publication rules and prompted debate about work opportunities, production costs, and industry change. Assessment requires separating statutory changes, possible consequences inferred from business mechanisms, and outcomes supported by data. [Cabinet Office: commencement and overview](https://www.gender.go.jp/magazine/backnumber/2022/520_r4_6_24.pdf)

## Which stages changed?

| Stage | Main change | Direct implication for the workflow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contracting | Delivery of contract and explanatory documents, with required explanations | Duties and rights must be specified in advance |
| Filming | At least one month after both contract and explanatory documents have been delivered | A newly contracted performer cannot immediately be filmed |
| Publication | At least four months after all filming ends, with an opportunity to review footage before publication | A statutory interval separates completed filming from permitted publication |
| Exit and distribution | Statutory termination rights and, where conditions are met, requests to stop sales or streaming | Agreeing to contract or film does not permanently remove exit and relief options |

This summarizes the current framework. Termination deadlines depend on the contract date; “two years after publication” does not apply to every contract. [Cabinet Office: key rules and current termination periods](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/index.html) See the [law article](../资料/法律/AV出演被害防止・救済法.md) for dates and conditions.

## Performers: choices and remedies they can exercise

The Cabinet Office explains that performers may refuse filming even when specified in the contract without liability for damages arising from that refusal. Producers must consider safety, hygiene, and freedom to refuse. Statutory termination also means that exit does not always require proving coercion at the outset. [Cabinet Office: legal commentary](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/pdf/houritsu_gaiyou.pdf)

This can matter both to people who chose the occupation and those who never wanted to perform. Signing, arriving on set, or completing filming cannot simply be treated as making everything irreversible. However, a right to request cessation does not automatically erase every online copy. Legal entitlement, notification, platform action, and removal of infringing copies are separate stages.

Practical effectiveness also depends on awareness, access to documents, and available help. Making a termination request does not by itself establish bad-faith conduct, while the existence of rights does not establish that coerced performances have disappeared.

## Producers: scheduling, finance, and allocation of risk

The timing rules imply a need to coordinate performers, locations, and release plans earlier. Projects relying on last-minute contracting for replacements need arrangements that already satisfy the conditions or a different filming date. Specific solutions require checking contracts and legal requirements. This is a workflow implication, not statistical evidence that the entire industry stopped filming.

If costs are paid during filming and revenue mainly follows publication, the waiting period may extend the time before invested funds return. The scale depends on completed inventory, advances, financing, and payment terms. More cautious selection of projects or performers is a plausible business hypothesis; widespread exclusion of newcomers or universal cash-flow failure still requires independent data.

**The publication waiting period is not a statutory delay in paying performers.** Cabinet Office commentary requires contracts to specify remuneration and payment timing. The four-month publication restriction cannot be restated as a legal requirement for actresses to wait four months for payment. [Cabinet Office: contractual remuneration terms](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/pdf/houritsu_gaiyou.pdf)

## Fewer jobs: what interviewees reported

In a Shueisha Online interview dated **18 December 2022**, male performer Shimiken linked disruption soon after commencement to insufficient industry preparation. This records a participant's observation of the transition, not the industry's condition years later. [Shueisha Online: interview with Shimiken](https://shueisha.online/articles/-/85032)

In a Nikkan SPA! discussion published on **6 March 2024**, Maiko Ayase and Aki Sasaki described work opportunities and restrictions on last-minute replacement bookings. These are some performers' occupational experiences, not an income survey of all performers. [Nikkan SPA!: performer discussion](https://nikkan-spa.jp/1981920)

“Fewer jobs” also requires separating filming sessions, bookings received by an individual, payment per booking, and annual earnings. Sales-page counts are not new-production counts: compilations, reissues, and alternative versions can change them. Without consistent definitions, a few interviews cannot establish the percentage by which an industry contracted.

## Underground activity, independent production, and overseas work

The same SPA! discussion mentioned overseas work and other production channels. Such secondhand accounts and observations raise risks worth investigating; they do not establish that the law caused every shift in employment. [Nikkan SPA!: accounts of changes in work](https://nikkan-spa.jp/1981920)

Establishing causation requires knowing who lost which bookings, when, and what work they actually took up, while examining demand, platform rules, debt, and personal choices. An event occurring after commencement does not establish that commencement caused it. Overseas activities can also include expos or photography and cannot all be labeled prostitution.

The Cabinet Office notes that individually produced material can fall within the framework. Applicability depends on statutory definitions and facts: “independent” or “private filming” labels do not automatically create exemptions. Using an overseas platform likewise does not establish compliance. [Cabinet Office: scope notice](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/index.html)

## What official data establish

Cabinet Office materials for an **October 2025** meeting report **500 consultations** concerning AV performance harm at one-stop support centers through the end of **fiscal 2024**, roughly two years and nine months after commencement, including **118** in that fiscal year. These are locally reported consultations, not 500 convictions or successful takedowns. [Cabinet Office: consultation and support figures, page 2](https://www.gender.go.jp/kaigi/sonota/pdf/avjkkaigi_2510_09.pdf)

Police materials for the same meeting record **41 cases cleared for violations of the law** between commencement of the penal provisions on **12 July 2022** and the end of **December 2024**. Police clearance is not conviction, and dividing this count by consultations does not produce a success rate. [National Police Agency: enforcement](https://www.gender.go.jp/kaigi/sonota/pdf/avjkkaigi_2510_10.pdf)

These records show consultation and enforcement mechanisms being used. They do not provide a comparison with what would have happened without the law. Consultation counts can reflect awareness, willingness to seek help, and changes in harm; an increase or decrease alone cannot establish success or failure.

Gaps are also officially documented. A government response dated **18 November 2025** stated that it did not track the conclusion of private performance contracts or related cancellations and did not then plan the investigation requested by the questioner. This describes that response, not an absence of all government information gathering. [House of Representatives: government response](https://www.shugiin.go.jp/internet/itdb_shitsumon.nsf/html/shitsumon/b219050.htm)

## Assessing future adjustments

Assessment should consider whether refusal and termination rights can actually be exercised, whether distribution stops promptly, and how bookings and income change for performers with different levels of experience. Examining protection and business burdens together does not make either a substitute for evidence about the other.

Arguments for shorter waiting periods, exceptions, or changed termination rules need to specify eligibility, protection against pressure, and ways to monitor harm. Arguments for retaining existing rules also need to address work conditions and access to support. Proposals, advocacy, and enacted provisions require separate verification.

As of the source review on **10 November 2026**, the cited material establishes institutional and some practical changes but is insufficient to quantify the law's net effect on industry-wide income, overseas work, or the incidence of harm.
