# AV actresses and drugs

“AV actresses and drugs” is an easy headline. Every few years, news breaks that an actress has been arrested for stimulants, cannabis or cocaine; media attach the label “former AV actress”, and social platforms inflate an individual case into an image of the industry. But an arrest report shows only what one person is suspected or found to have done. It does not show how many people in the profession use drugs. This article reviews the law, individual cases and industry practice that the public record can establish. It does not speculate about anyone’s private life, treats no single case as the industry norm, and is not legal or medical advice.

## Distinguishing use, being drugged, possession and arrest

“Drugs” is a vague everyday word; in law, different substances and different acts are separated. At least four situations need to be distinguished:

- **Use**: the person ingests the drug. This does not establish addiction, voluntariness or duration.
- **Being drugged**: someone gives the drug without the person’s knowledge. This is harm to the victim, not the person’s own use.
- **Possession**: the person manages or carries the drug. It does not prove use, sale or organised-crime links.
- **Arrest / indictment**: the investigation and prosecution stage. It is not a conviction; before judgment, the person is a suspect.

In November 2026, a former AV actress and current DJ living in Hong Kong had convulsions while working in Tsim Sha Tsui and was taken to hospital, where tests showed reactions to four drugs. Her manager told the media that she may have drunk something a customer gave her, and police are investigating a 61-year-old man. [Sankei: former AV actress taken to hospital in Hong Kong](https://www.sankei.com/article/20261101-GQGDOS5WNBLIZIP22Y753QU7BA/) This is a suspected drug-spiking case, not an “actress using drugs” case. Confusing the two harms the victim again and distracts from the real issue of use.

| Situation | Meaning | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Use | The person ingested the drug | Addiction, voluntariness or frequency |
| Being drugged | Another person administered it without the person’s knowledge | The person’s own use or consent |
| Possession | The person managed or carried the drug | Use, sale or organised-crime links |
| Arrest / indictment | Investigation and prosecution stage | Guilt; before judgment the person is a suspect |

A further distinction is the content of the work itself. AV titles may depict drug use or abuse, but that is performance and product, not a record of the set; the theme cannot be used to infer that a performer used drugs.

## How strict Japanese law is

Japan imposes severe penalties for drug offences. According to the Tokyo Metropolitan Police, possession or use of stimulants carries up to 10 years’ imprisonment; heroin and other narcotics up to 10 years; cannabis, cocaine, MDMA, ketamine and LSD up to 7 years. From 12 December 2024, cannabis has been classified as a “narcotic” and is punished more severely. [Tokyo Metropolitan Police: warning on drug offences](https://www.keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/multilingual/japanese/safe_society/law_violations/warning.html)

These are only the statutory maxima for simple possession or use; commercial purposes, smuggling and organised crime carry heavier penalties. Overall, Japanese police recorded 13,462 drug offenders in 2024; stimulants accounted for 45.5%, cannabis for 45.1%, and organised-crime group members and associates for 17.4%. [National Police Agency: 2025 white paper on crime, chapter 4, section 4.1](https://www.npa.go.jp/hakusyo/r07/honbun/html/bb4441000.html)

These figures show that drug problems are widespread in society, not unique to the AV industry. They also show that a news report saying “an AV actress was arrested” cannot establish the prevalence of use in the profession, because there is no occupational breakdown and no reliable denominator for the total number of AV actresses.

## What the public cases can show

The cases below are limited to those reported by major media whose basic facts can be confirmed. They are not a complete list and do not represent the industry.

**Rumina Yuki**. In September 2021, the former Miss Gakushuin and AV actress Rumina Yuki was arrested for violating the Stimulants Control Act and later received a two-year prison sentence, suspended. In a 2022 interview, she said that in 2019 a man she was dating gave her cannabis disguised as a cigarette, and that over the following four days she also used cocaine and stimulants. She said she could not refuse, and that drug use and sex were intertwined. In 2024 she spoke again about leaving dependence and about her awareness work. [Bunshun Online: Rumina Yuki interview](https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/56403) [Nikkan SPA!: Rumina Yuki on arrest and recovery](https://nikkan-spa.jp/2056132)

This is her own account, not a court finding. But it shows that “use” can be entangled with intimacy, deception, coercion and sex; it is not a simple “personal choice”.

**Nozomi Aso**. In 2016, the former AV actress Nozomi Aso received a sentence of one year and six months, suspended for three years, for cocaine and other offences. In 2018 she was arrested again for possession and use of stimulants and received one year and eight months, of which four months were suspended and the rest to be served. The judgment noted her “subordination” (dependence and submission) to the man she was then dating, but also found that she had reoffended while on probation and possessed a relatively large amount. [Bengo4: Nozomi Aso receives a prison sentence](https://www.bengo4.com/c_1009/c_1297/n_8890/)

The case shows that repeated use can escalate to prison. Courts can recognise control within a relationship, but control is not a defence.

**Serina Shin**. In July 2026, the AV actress Serina Shin (27) was arrested with a senior member of a Yamaguchi-gumi-affiliated organised crime group at a hotel in Toshima, Tokyo, on suspicion of possessing and using cocaine. Reports said about 0.7 grams of cocaine were found; the man admitted the allegations and the woman denied use. At the time of reporting, the case was still in the judicial process. [News Post Seven: Serina Shin arrested](https://www.news-postseven.com/archives/20260826_2128043.html)

The case shows that drug cases can intersect with organised crime. It also shows that arrest and indictment are not conviction, and that a denial is a fact that should be recorded.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2021 | Rumina Yuki arrested for possessing stimulants | Two-year sentence, suspended; later spoke about recovery | Dependence can be entangled with intimacy, deception and coercion |
| 2016/2018 | Nozomi Aso sentenced twice, for cocaine and stimulants | Suspended in 2016; partly served in 2018 | Repeated use can escalate to prison; courts can see subordination |
| 2026 | Serina Shin arrested on suspicion of cocaine possession; she denies use | Case in the judicial process | Arrest is not guilt; organised crime may be involved |
| 2026 | Former AV actress and DJ drugged in Hong Kong | Hospitalised; police investigation | Being drugged is not use; victims are drawn in too |

## From “personal downfall” to control and coercion

It is easiest to describe drug problems as “personal downfall”, but that does not explain why some people first use under another person’s inducement, deception or pressure.

In Yuki’s account, cannabis was passed off as a cigarette, and cocaine and stimulants followed within four days. She said she could not refuse, and that drug use and sex were intertwined. This is not a claim that she bears no responsibility. It is a claim that “consent” depends on information, capacity and the option to say no.

The Aso judgment also referred to her “subordination” to the man she was dating. The court took that into account in sentencing, but did not avoid a prison term. Courts can recognise control, but control is not a defence.

Drugs can also be used as a means of control: taking away the other person’s judgment, creating dependence, making it hard to leave; or binding a relationship through “using together”. These mechanisms can occur in intimate relationships, the sex industry and nightlife; they are not unique to AV.

But one point must be clear: being coerced, drugged or controlled does not automatically mean innocence. Whether an offence is committed, and how much responsibility attaches, is for the courts to decide on the facts. This article does not re-try judgments or conclude that any person is innocent or guilty of a serious offence.

## Why the AV industry is easily drawn in

The AV industry has structural features that make drug risk more visible and potentially more likely.

First, **high visibility and stigma**. Once arrested, a performer is permanently labelled a “former AV actress”; the case is read as a problem of the industry rather than of an individual.

Second, **project-based freelance work**. Income and work are unstable; stopping work stops income. Psychological support is thin, and the cost of asking for help is high.

Third, **movement between nightlife work**. Some public cases involve people who also work in hostess clubs, DJing or events. These settings can increase contact with drug distribution, but they do not allow inferences about the whole industry.

Fourth, **privacy and online abuse**. Exposure affects family, contracts, fans and future employment; many people cannot ask for help publicly.

These are risk factors, not proof of causation. There is no data showing that AV actresses use drugs at a higher rate than the general population. Treating risk factors as “inevitable” is stereotyping.

## The industry has no unified defence

This article found no unified drug-testing system in the AV industry. Whether an individual agency writes a prohibition into its contract, requires testing, or terminates a contract after a case depends on the individual contract and the company; there is no public industry standard.

The AV new law mainly regulates performance contracts, acts during filming and relief for performers. In the text and commentary reviewed here, no provision making drug testing or drug use a mandatory disclosure item was found. [Cabinet Office: explanation of the AV Act](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/pdf/seiritsu_kaisetsu.pdf), [full text of the Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/504AC1000000078) In other words, the law treats drug use as a criminal matter, while prevention, testing, education and return-to-work support within the industry are largely voluntary.

Some companies terminate contracts or stop working with a performer after a scandal; that is a commercial decision, not a legal penalty. For the performer, criminal punishment, lost contracts and media exposure can arrive at the same time. The public record also contains no treatment or return-to-work rates for AV actresses with drug dependence; neither “arrest is the end” nor “release means comeback” can be assumed.

## Media labels and the “AV actress = drugs” stereotype

Arrest reports use labels such as “former AV actress” or “セクシー女優” (sexy actress) even when the person retired or changed careers long ago. This encourages readers to read an individual case as a trait of the profession.

Exposure bias is also strong: arrests are reported, but use that does not lead to arrest is not; people who recover do not become news. Estimating prevalence from the number of reports therefore systematically overstates it.

There is no denominator: we do not know the total number of AV actresses, or how many of them use drugs. Claims such as “80%” or “many” have no data behind them. The number of reported drug-crime cases is limited; without a denominator, no proportion can be given. A few cases should not be treated as the whole, and they do not prove the industry is “clean” either.

## Legal and professional consequences

Arrest brings consequences beyond punishment.

- **Investigation and detention**: detention, indictment and trial take time.
- **Punishment**: stimulants carry up to 10 years; a first offence may be suspended, but repeated use or reoffending while on probation may lead to prison.
- **Contracts**: agencies and studios may terminate contracts, stop sales or remove titles; there is no standard compensation.
- **Media**: real names, stage names and old titles remain linked; even after a comeback, search results remain.
- **Daily life**: employment, housing and immigration screening may be affected; the specifics vary by case.

These consequences weigh especially heavily on project-based performers, who have no salary, paid leave or company benefits and bear everything personally. But another exaggeration should be avoided: arrest does not mean permanent exclusion from society. Japan has treatment and support resources, and the difficulty of returning varies by case.

## Recovery, treatment and support

Drug dependence is a treatable health problem, not simply a moral failure. Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare runs an addiction portal with information on consultation services and medical institutions. [Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare: addiction portal](https://www.izonsho.mhlw.go.jp/)

In a 2024 interview, Rumina Yuki said that a regular life, exercise, psychological support and a new relationship helped her leave dependence, and that she is engaged in awareness work. Treatment usually includes psychotherapy, medication (for withdrawal or co-occurring conditions), peer support and family support; the specific plan is for a specialist service to decide. This article does not offer medical advice.

For the industry, real prevention is not only terminating contracts after a scandal. It is explanation before contracting, drug education at work, anonymous consultation, treatment support during time off, and non-discrimination on return. The public record contains no systematic data on whether such measures exist.

For readers, the important point is not to turn “drug use” into a moral spectacle, and not to treat a person who was drugged as a user. Both require facts, not speculation.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Arrest means guilt.”** Arrest and indictment are procedures; guilt and sentence are for a court to decide.
- **“One AV actress used drugs, so the whole industry does.”** An individual case does not represent the industry; without a denominator and a sample, no rate can be calculated.
- **“Being drugged is the same as using.”** Being drugged is harm by another person, not the person’s use or consent.
- **“Cannabis is not a drug and is safe.”** It is illegal in Japan; since December 2024 it has been regulated as a narcotic, and it carries health risks.
- **“The AV industry has unified drug testing.”** This article found no unified system; individual contracts may differ, but that is not an industry standard.
- **“A strong will is all it takes to stop.”** Dependence is a treatable health problem requiring professional support, not a contest of moral will.
- **“The public cases are the whole picture.”** Only reported cases are visible; non-users, people in recovery and people never detected do not appear in the news.

## What the sources show

What this article can confirm: Japan imposes severe penalties for drug offences; police recorded 13,462 drug offenders in 2024; the AV industry has reported cases of use, possession and arrest; some cases involve deception, control or subordination in intimate relationships; some involve drug-spiking of a victim; some intersect with organised crime; there is no public unified drug-testing system in the AV industry; the AV new law does not make drug testing a mandatory disclosure item; dependence is treatable and recovery is possible; media labels amplify individual cases.

What it cannot confirm: the prevalence of drug use among AV actresses, trends, average sentences, treatment rates, return-to-work rates, the share of agencies that test, or a causal relationship between AV work and drug use. Arrest reports, self-accounts, media selection and court judgments all carry selection bias; without a denominator, “the cases we see” cannot be turned into “the rate for the whole”.

As of the source-check date, **17 November 2026**, no representative survey of drug use in the AV industry was found. Any general claim that “this is how AV actresses are” therefore goes beyond what the available public sources can support.
