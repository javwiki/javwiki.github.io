# AV actresses and cosmetic surgery

AV turns faces and bodies directly into products. Viewers see a cover, a still or a short clip before deciding whether to open a title, and studios make the same judgement at casting: will this person look right on camera? In that sense, cosmetic surgery is not only a private aesthetic choice. It is an occupational variable that can affect access to work, the roles offered, shooting schedules and income.

Public discussion, however, tends to focus on whether a particular actress has had surgery rather than on how the industry uses it. Covers are retouched; makeup, lighting, lenses and age all change appearance; and a performer may choose to disclose or stay silent. This article reviews the industry practices, individual cases and limits of the public record. It does not determine whether any named person has had surgery, and it is not medical advice.

## Distinguishing cosmetic surgery, cosmetic medicine and photo retouching

The Chinese word “整容” usually means surgery that changes the shape of the face or body, corresponding to the Japanese “美容整形”. Japanese media and official documents more often use “美容医療” (cosmetic medicine), a broader category that includes surgery, injections, lasers, hair removal and skin treatment. This article deals with cosmetic medicine intended to change appearance, not reconstructive surgery after injury or for congenital conditions. Most cosmetic medicine in Japan is self-pay (“自由診療”) and is not covered by public health insurance. [Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare: handling of cosmetic medicine](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/newpage_65283.html)

The face a viewer sees on screen has passed through at least four layers: makeup and hair, lighting and lens, camera angle and editing, and retouching of covers and promotional images. In 2015, a designer who had worked at an AV production company said in an interview that covers are retouched. He added that at his company the actresses were already attractive, so major changes were not needed. He also recalled a client asking for a B cup to be changed to an F cup. His advice for spotting the gap was to compare the front and back covers: on low-budget titles, the back cover often receives less retouching. [LIG: a former AV production designer on cover design](https://liginc.co.jp/217612/2)

This does not make retouching the same as surgery. It means that **a difference between the cover and the film is not evidence of surgery on its own**. Changes between an old title and a new one can also come from weight, age, orthodontics, makeup, focal length or a different shooting style.

| Material | What it can show | What it cannot show |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covers, gravure and social-media photos | A selected, lit and retouched image intended to be seen | Whether surgery took place, or which procedure |
| Visible change on screen | That appearance has changed | Whether the cause was surgery, makeup, weight, age or editing |
| The performer’s own disclosure | The part of her experience she chose to make public | A full medical record, or anything about anyone else |
| Agency or contract | That an individual contract may constrain appearance | Any published industry-wide standard |
| Clinic advertising and before/after images | Commercial promotion | The industry as a whole, or safety |

## Why the AV industry is closely tied to cosmetic surgery

AV sells titles, but the entry point to a title is a face. Covers, platform thumbnails, short social-media clips and event photos compete for a few seconds of attention. At casting, appearance is one of the first filters. It determines not only whether someone is “attractive enough” but whether she is selected, what roles she is offered and whether she can obtain an exclusive contract.

In 2018, the journalist Atsuhiko Nakamura recorded the experience of a 23-year-old woman (a pseudonym) in Gendai Business. She applied to an AV agency and was told at the interview that she would be “difficult”. The interviewer said that if she had surgery, she “might be able to appear in kikaku titles”, and told her, in effect, “if you want to appear, get your whole face done”, at a cost of 2 million yen. She was working in the sex industry at the time and paid for the surgery from those earnings. [Gendai Business: cosmetic surgery to become an AV actress](https://gendai.media/articles/-/54586)

The same article said that AV agencies work with cosmetic surgery clinics; that agencies used to pay or lend the cost as an investment, but by then it had become “the woman’s own responsibility and her own investment”; and that if she had surgery and passed the threshold, the agency would introduce her to work. This is one interviewee’s account, reported by one journalist, not an industry-wide survey. But it reveals the structure: **surgery is framed as a personal investment, the risk sits with the individual, and the return must first pass through industry screening.**

In 2025, the former actress and freelance writer Aaya Takanashi described the industry she had observed in Nikkan SPA!. She wrote that it would not be an exaggeration to say about 80% of AV actresses have done some “face maintenance”; double-eyelid surgery using the buried-suture method is routine; bone contouring and prostheses in the nose, chin and forehead are too numerous to list; and many who have no surgery still concentrate on skin treatments, so “one could say almost all have had something done”. She also wrote that repeated surgery can make a face look unnatural and leave old fans asking “who is this?” [Nikkan SPA!: a former actress on actresses and cosmetic surgery](https://nikkan-spa.jp/2103278)

Her account matters, but its limits must be stated. It is one industry insider’s impression, not a sample survey. “Eighty percent” is not a statistic, and the actresses she encountered may not represent the whole industry.

In the wider context, Japan’s cosmetic medicine market was worth about 631 billion yen in 2024, up 6.2% year on year. The research firm attributed growth to a lower psychological barrier, more non-surgical treatments and rising demand from men. [Yano Research Institute: cosmetic medicine market](https://www.yano.co.jp/press-release/show/press_id/3844) The AV industry is not an exception to that trend; it is a setting that magnifies both the occupational return and the occupational risk of investing in appearance.

## Before debut, after debut, and “re-debuting”

Cosmetic surgery enters an AV career at three broad points.

The first is **before debut**, as a ticket into the industry. The 23-year-old in the 2018 case was first told that she would be “difficult” as she was, then given a route to try after surgery, along with a price. The issue is not only the cost, but who defines the “pass mark” and how far she can refuse.

The second is **after debut**, as maintenance and upgrading. たかなし wrote that actresses who can get work earn more and can take longer breaks, so they can keep returning to clinics. What begins as a wish to improve one feature can become a maintenance loop that “does not stop”. Some, she added, treat surgery as a hobby and the next operation as motivation for work.

The third is **a new stage name after a break**, combining a change in appearance with a change of name to rebuild the audience’s first impression. Eimi Fukada is a case in which this path was discussed publicly.

According to Sports Nippon in May 2026, Fukada debuted at 18 as 天海心 (天海こころ) but her titles did not sell. She saved 3 million yen over two years and went to South Korea. On the programme, she said she stayed a week at the clinic and had the “full” set done: eyes, nose, cheekbones and V-line. As a monitor she received a reduced price and paid 3 million yen. She rested for a month after surgery and then re-debuted as Eimi Fukada. “The reason I entered AV was that I wanted cosmetic surgery,” she said. [Sports Nippon: Eimi Fukada on full-face surgery](https://www.sponichi.co.jp/entertainment/news/2026/05/29/kiji/20260529s00041000137000c.html)

Another actress who has spoken publicly is Kirara Asuka. In April 2022, on a Fuji Television variety programme, she said “I’ve had most of it done”, explaining that she had changed her eyes, nose and mouth, made her face smaller, and had regular thread lifts; her first surgery was about 15 years earlier, beginning with hyaluronic acid to raise her nose. [Nishispo: Kirara Asuka on cosmetic surgery](https://nishispo.nishinippon.co.jp/article/1016953) She also opened a cosmetic clinic she produced, TOKYO ACE CLINIC, in 2019. [Modelpress: the cosmetic clinic produced by Kirara Asuka](https://mdpr.jp/news/detail/1878078)

What the two have in common is that both disclosed after becoming known in the industry, and the disclosure itself became part of their personal brand. It does not follow that other actresses will disclose, still less that their choices are the industry norm.

| Timing | Typical motive | Publicly visible evidence | Main risks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Before debut | To pass casting and obtain work | Individual interviews, agency statements | Cost and debt, a “pass mark” defined by others, recovery time |
| After debut | To maintain an image, extend a career, relieve anxiety | Former actresses’ columns, the performer’s own social media | Repeated surgery, lost work, contract conflict, health risk |
| Re-debut | To reset the audience’s impression and accompany a name change | The performer’s own programme appearances, media reports | Old titles remain, audience comparison, failing again |

## Agencies, exclusive contracts and “unauthorised” surgery

An AV actress’s relationship with her agency, and with production companies, is usually not a single employment relationship. An exclusive actress signs an exclusive contract with a studio and appears at a fixed pace; a kikaku-tantai actress appears for several studios at once. Appearance, hairstyle and hair colour are sometimes written into contracts or work arrangements. たかなし wrote that an exclusive actress must maintain a certain appearance during her contract, and that even the presence of a fringe, hair length and colour may be specified. If she has major surgery (breast augmentation, for example) without consulting anyone, the recovery time leaves a gap in the shooting schedule and the manager can only apologise to the production side. If she is not a big seller, the contract may be terminated; if she is, she may only be told to “consult us next time”. [Nikkan SPA!: page 2](https://nikkan-spa.jp/2103278/2)

This account points to something often missed: **surgery is a decision about the body, but also about schedules and contracts.** Recovery, follow-up appointments and possible complications affect shoots that are already booked. What the production side is buying is an appearance that will be available at a particular time; a sudden change undermines that predictability.

But this too is one former insider’s account. The public record contains no industry-wide contract template and no statistic on what share of contracts restrict surgery. Terms can differ greatly between agencies, studios, exclusive and kikaku arrangements.

On the law, the AV performer protection and relief law (the “AV new law”) mainly sets out pre-contract disclosure duties, limits on acts during filming and contract cancellation. The matters to be explained include the content of the filming, remuneration and STI testing. In the text and commentary reviewed here, no provision making cosmetic surgery a mandatory disclosure or notification item was found. [Cabinet Office: explanation of the AV Act](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/pdf/seiritsu_kaisetsu.pdf), [full text of the Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/504AC1000000078) Whether to tell an agency, and what happens if one does not, therefore depends mainly on the individual contract and internal rules.

## Who pays: self-funding, advances and “surgery as investment”

Who bears the cost is central to understanding the practice.

The 2018 report said the arrangement had already become one in which the woman bore the cost and invested in herself; the earlier practice of agencies paying or lending had declined. The 23-year-old returned to sex work to pay 2 million yen for surgery. [Gendai Business](https://gendai.media/articles/-/54586) Fukada saved 3 million yen from two years of AV income, went to South Korea, and received a discount as a monitor. [Sports Nippon](https://www.sponichi.co.jp/entertainment/news/2026/05/29/kiji/20260529s00041000137000c.html)

These two paths show that the money can come from the performer’s savings, other work, or an advance from an agency. The public cases show no single “the agency pays” model. There are also no industry-wide figures for average cost, debt or the share of advances; what can be confirmed is that both self-funding and individual advances have existed, and that arrangements can differ by period and company.

Calling surgery an “investment” is understandable at the individual level: if appearance brings more work, the cost may be recovered. But the phrase obscures three things.

1. **The return is uncertain.** Surgery does not guarantee selection or success; the stories that become visible tend to be those of people who succeeded.
2. **The cost is not only the operation.** There is lost income during recovery, follow-up appointments, treatment for complications, revision surgery and possibly long-term maintenance.
3. **The risk sits with the individual.** If surgery fails or a career does not take off, the debt and the health consequences remain with her; the industry does not automatically share them.

The cosmetic medicine consultations recorded by Japan’s National Consumer Affairs Center include people who were pressed to sign on the day, high-priced contracts, asymmetry and scarring after a lift, and drooping eyelids after injections. [National Consumer Affairs Center: cosmetic medicine services](https://www.kokusen.go.jp/soudan_topics/data/biyo.html) These cases are not specific to AV actresses, but they show that “surgery as investment” is not always a calculable transaction.

## Health risks and the underestimated downtime

Every operation carries risks: bleeding, infection, anaesthetic accidents, scarring, asymmetry, nerve damage and thrombosis. Injections and energy-based treatments are not “risk-free” either. The consumer cases include lumps after injections, asymmetry and scarring after a face lift, and drooping eyelids after Botox in the forehead and between the brows. More serious complications are rare, but they are not zero. [National Consumer Affairs Center](https://www.kokusen.go.jp/soudan_topics/data/biyo.html)

Breast augmentation is another procedure often mentioned in the industry, and one of the areas where caution matters most. In 2019, Japanese societies related to cosmetic medicine issued a joint statement that, until safety was demonstrated, non-absorbable fillers should not be injected for breast augmentation; the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare has also warned consumers on that basis. [Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare: checks before cosmetic treatment](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/newpage_04978.html) “Non-absorbable” means the material is not absorbed by the body, which makes problems harder to deal with.

Downtime is also underestimated. Fukada said she rested for a month after surgery; たかなし wrote that larger operations require longer recovery and shoots have to be pushed back. AV work is largely project-based, so time off usually means no income; if complications need treatment, medical costs and lost earnings add up. This article found no statistic on the complication rate among AV actresses and no industry-wide reporting system. “I have never heard of a case” is therefore not evidence of low risk.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare offers four checks for anyone considering cosmetic medicine: can you explain the drug or material yourself; do you know the risks and side effects as well as the benefits and are you satisfied; have you been told about alternatives and made your own choice; and is it really necessary “now”? [Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/newpage_04978.html) The list is not specific to AV actresses, but it applies just the same.

## Disclosure, silence and judging from photos

The clearly disclosed cases found for this article are few. Kirara Asuka spoke in 2022 and Eimi Fukada in 2026; the media called such statements a “shocking confession”, which itself shows that disclosure remains the exception rather than the norm.

There are many reasons not to disclose. Medical information is private; disclosure can affect family, contracts and commercial work; and an audience may keep comparing a performer with “her old face”. The reasons to disclose are equally varied: some make it part of a personal brand, some want to reduce speculation, and some want to talk about the cost of surgery. **Disclosing or not disclosing is not a moral judgement in itself.** Disclosure is not the whole truth, and silence is not a lie.

On the audience side, the easiest boundary to cross is “judging from photos”. An old title, a new title, an event photo, a social-media selfie and a retouched magazine image all come from different conditions; the same face looks different through different lenses. Placing two images side by side can show that someone “looks different”, not what operation she had. Nor can one conclude that she had surgery because she looks better, or that she is lying because she denies it.

In fan culture, “who is this?” and “she’s completely changed” are common reactions. For a profession that sells faces, those reactions directly affect evaluations and sales. たかなし also wrote that too great a change can drive old fans away. On the other hand, repeated speculation, “surgery diagnosis” videos and questioning a performer on her own account can easily turn discussion into harassment. **It is possible to discuss an industry phenomenon without performing a medical diagnosis on a particular person.**

## Why the face pays so well

Zooming out, cosmetic surgery in AV is not simply “actresses being vain”. There are at least three structural reasons.

First, **appearance is the cheapest screening criterion.** Studios have to decide quickly whether a newcomer can be filmed and what types of role she can take; face and body are the most immediate information. Viewers also decide within seconds whether to click. Appearance therefore becomes the form of capital that is easiest to quantify and easiest to compare.

Second, **investment in appearance is positional.** If only a few people have surgery, it can confer an advantage. As more do, the industry’s “average face” rises, and those who might otherwise have passed may be pushed down in comparison. This is an arms race: everyone invests more, but relative position may not improve.

Third, **costs are individualised while returns are commodified.** The operation, the recovery and the complications are borne by the performer; the revenue generated by the title, the cover and the attention enters the sales chain of the production side and the platform first. Calling all of this “personal choice” or “self-investment” obscures the industry’s collective demand for a “passing appearance”.

The same logic appears in gravure, idol and hostess work; AV is not unique. What differs is that AV images remain on platforms for a long time, so changes in appearance are permanently recorded and compared. Surgery is therefore both a career strategy and a public trace that cannot be fully withdrawn.

## Common misconceptions

- **“More than 80% of AV actresses have had surgery.”** The source is a former actress’s 2025 column saying about 80% have done “face maintenance”; it is an impression, not a sample survey, and it includes non-surgical treatments. She gave no sample, definition or method.
- **“If the cover and the film differ, she had surgery.”** Covers are retouched, lit and angled; the former production designer said retouching happens and gave an example of a cup size being changed.
- **“Surgery always makes an actress more successful.”** Only the success stories are visible; those who fail may leave the industry and decline interviews.
- **“The agency always pays.”** The 2018 report said self-funding was the main pattern at the time; advances and loans existed earlier, but current proportions by company are unknown.
- **“Disclosure means honesty; silence means lying.”** Medical information is private, and disclosure can be a brand strategy. Neither proves the whole truth.
- **“You can tell from a photo.”** You cannot. Retouching, makeup, lighting, lenses, weight and age all change appearance.
- **“Surgery has no risks and recovery takes a few days.”** Surgery and injections both carry complications and downtime; non-absorbable fillers for breast augmentation are specifically flagged by Japanese medical societies and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.

## What the sources show

What this article can confirm: the AV industry is markedly appearance-centred, and both casting and sales are affected; there are reports of agencies advising or requiring applicants to have surgery, with a price and a “pass mark”; there are cases of actresses paying for surgery themselves, including travel to South Korea, and re-debuting under a new name; a former actress describes repeated surgery, shoots being rescheduled after unannounced operations, and contract risk; covers and promotional images are retouched; Japan’s cosmetic medicine market is growing, and consumer consultations include complications and high-priced contracts; and the AV new law, as reviewed here, does not make cosmetic surgery a mandatory disclosure item.

What it cannot confirm: the proportion of AV actresses who have surgery, average costs, debt levels, complication rates, the share of agency advances, the share of contracts that restrict surgery, or the causal effect of surgery on career success. Former actresses’ columns, individual interviews, clinic advertising and fan observation all carry selection bias: those willing to speak, those who are noticed, those who succeed and those who are harmed are not random samples.

As of the source-check date, **17 November 2026**, no representative survey of cosmetic surgery in the AV industry was found. Any general claim that “this is how AV actresses do surgery” therefore goes beyond what the available public sources can support.
