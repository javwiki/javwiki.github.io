# AV actresses and the fūzoku industry

“AV actress” and “fūzoku industry” are often treated as the same thing, but they differ in law, working method and income structure. AV is the production and sale of filmed content; fūzoku is a licensed or notified service business, delivered in shops or by dispatch. There is nevertheless movement of people, money and names between them: some people enter AV from fūzoku, some work in fūzoku alongside AV, and some shops advertise “AV actress on the roster”. This article maps those interfaces and the public cases. It does not speculate about anyone’s private life and does not treat individual cases as the industry norm.

## Distinguishing AV from the fūzoku industry

In Japanese law, “fūzoku eigyō” (風俗営業) is broad: it includes social restaurants (cabaret clubs and similar), low-light restaurants, partitioned restaurants, mahjong parlours and arcades. Sex-related businesses fall under the broader category of “sex-related special businesses” (性風俗関連特殊営業), including soaplands, fashion health clubs, delivery health (deriheru), strip theatres, love hotels and adult shops. [Tokyo Metropolitan Police: list of fūzoku business types](https://www.keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/tetsuzuki/fuzoku/gyoshu_ichiran.html) In other words, the Chinese term “風俗業” often means the sex industry, but the Japanese legal term “風俗営業” is not all sexual.

AV is not a fūzoku business; it is the production and sale of filmed content. It has its own contracts, review, distribution and age-verification process, and is governed by the AV performer protection and relief law (the “AV new law”) and other rules. Fūzoku businesses are subject to the Entertainment Businesses Act (風営法) and prefectural ordinances, including licences or notifications, area, time and advertising restrictions. [Full text: Entertainment Businesses Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/323AC0000000122) The regulators, legal basis and risk profiles differ.

| Dimension | AV | Fūzoku | Commonly confused point |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legal framework | Filmed production, distribution and performance contracts; AV new law, Criminal Code, child pornography law | Licences or notifications under the Entertainment Businesses Act; service range differs by business type | “AV = fūzoku” does not hold |
| Income | Per title or contract; project-based and volatile | Per shift or service; daily or monthly, with faster cash flow | One industry’s income cannot represent the other |
| Customer relationship | Viewers buy titles; usually no direct contact | Customers visit or receive a dispatch; direct contact | Fans’ desire to “meet” is a different issue |
| Publicity | Stage name and titles remain long-term | Stage name and shop name; some shops hide real identity | A title can cross industries, but the work differs |
| Regulatory focus | Content review, age verification, contract explanation | Public safety commission licences, area and time limits, advertising limits | Legality does not mean zero risk |

## From fūzoku to AV: a publicly described route

In 2022, CBC Radio reported the experience of a 29-year-old former AV actress, C. She began working at a delivery health (deriheru) shop at 21; at 23, a staff member at the shop sent her CV to a major AV agency; she passed an interview and debuted in AV. She said there was no coercion; she decided because it “seemed interesting”. She worked for two years; each title paid about 100,000 yen, of which the agency took 10%, leaving her about 90,000 yen. [CBC Radio: a former AV actress describes entering the industry](https://radichubu.jp/makozuba/contents/id=43830)

This is one publicly described route: a fūzoku shop staff member or scout → an AV agency → interview and contract → debut. It does not mean that all AV actresses come from fūzoku, or that all fūzoku workers want to make AV. But it shows that a recruitment channel exists between the two markets.

The risks in this route are that the recruiter may profit from both the shop and the agency; that the person may underestimate the publicity, contract term and physical demands of AV; and that a “just try it” decision may be made with incomplete information.

## From AV to fūzoku: the premium on a title

In 2016, Sankei reported a case in Yoshiwara, Tokyo. A soapland popular as a shop “with AV actresses on the roster” was raided; police then investigated three presidents of entertainment agencies on suspicion of violating the Employment Security Act (introducing women to harmful work). They were suspected of referring three actresses to the shop. Nikkei reported that police also suspected some were made to work against their will. [Sankei: from AV actress to soapland](https://www.sankei.com/article/20161128-MTGNG544VBKDXEITADNTBOSFDY/) [Nikkei: agency executives investigated](https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGKKASDG21HGN_R21C16A1CC1000/)

The same report described the business model: the shop posted actresses’ names, photos and titles on its website; 120 minutes normally cost 65,000 yen, while a “popular actress” was priced at 80,000 yen as “premier”; the shop took 15,000 yen; the three agencies had between 40 and 120 actresses. The shop’s sales over about seven years were said to be about 1.029 billion yen.

In 2021, Bunshun interviewed a 24-year-old music college student (pseudonym Ishihara Yuna). She was a kikaku-tantai AV actress earning about 300,000 yen a month from AV; at the same time she worked at a high-class soapland in Yoshiwara, earning about 600,000 yen a month. “Because I had the AV actress title, I could become a popular girl,” she said. After the pandemic reduced both AV shoots and shop traffic, she began パパ活 (paid dating). [Bunshun Online: an AV actress working at a soapland](https://bunshun.jp/articles/-/49872)

These cases show that an AV title can be converted into customers and price in the fūzoku market. But the conversion is not automatic, and income is not guaranteed. It depends on the shop’s promotion, the actress’s fame and fans’ desire to “meet the person”. The 2016 case also shows that when referrals between agencies, shops and actresses involve sexual services, they can fall under the Employment Security Act or the Anti-Prostitution Act; the boundary between “consent” and “being made to work” becomes the focus of investigation.

## Why working in both is common

There are at least three structural reasons.

First, **income volatility**. AV is project-based and unstable; fūzoku is paid by shift or service, with faster cash flow. The 2016 report said kikaku actresses’ fees had fallen to 30,000–50,000 yen; in the 2021 case, AV brought about 300,000 yen a month and the soapland about 600,000 yen. These are individual cases, not averages, but they show the difference in income structure.

Second, **the premium on a title**. An AV actress’s fame brings bookings to a fūzoku shop. Shops use “AV actress on the roster” in advertising, and actresses can use the title to raise prices or attract customers. The more famous the actress, the larger the premium; for an unknown actress, the effect is limited.

Third, **recruitment and proximity**. AV agencies, fūzoku shops, scouts, events and social media are connected. Routes exist from fūzoku to AV and from AV to fūzoku, and schedules and locations can be staggered. In 2018, an industry article in Nikkan SOD described both AV actresses and fūzoku women as becoming “idol-like”: AV actresses do events, gravure and social media; fūzoku women write photo diaries and manage bookings. [Nikkan SOD: the “idolisation” of AV actresses and fūzoku women](https://news.sod.co.jp/eroneta/25275/)

But this is not evidence that “all AV actresses work in fūzoku”. There is no representative survey covering both industries in the public record; what is visible is individual cases, police cases and shop advertising, all selective.

## Agencies, contracts and the AV new law

Contracts between an AV actress and her agency, or with a studio, may restrict other sexual work or require disclosure. Fūzoku shops also have rules on shifts, stage names, prohibited acts and testing. There is no public industry-wide standard; terms depend on the individual contract and company.

The AV new law mainly sets out explanation, cooling-off and cancellation for AV performance contracts, and limits on acts during filming. It does not regulate fūzoku. Fūzoku is governed by the Entertainment Businesses Act, the Anti-Prostitution Act, the Employment Security Act and other rules. The protection of the AV new law cannot simply be applied to fūzoku work. [Cabinet Office: explanation of the AV Act](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/pdf/seiritsu_kaisetsu.pdf), [full text: AV Act](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/504AC1000000078)

If an agency refers an actress to a fūzoku shop and profits from it, it may fall under the Employment Security Act’s prohibition on introducing women to harmful work; the 2016 case is an example. Whether it is illegal depends on the referral, the work, consent and how the profit is made. For the performer, whether side work is allowed by contract, whether disclosure is required, how income is declared, and who pays for insurance and health checks are matters to confirm individually. The public record contains no industry-wide answer.

## The business of “AV actress on the roster”

Fūzoku shops use “AV actress on the roster” because it converts an image that is “out of reach” into a service that is “meetable”. The editor quoted in the 2016 Sankei report said that “an AV actress you can go and meet” has dream value for male customers. This is the commercialisation of a title: the shop borrows the title, and the actress borrows the customer base.

But a “roster” listing is not necessarily true. A shop may use old photos, an old stage name, unauthorised photos, or even invent an “AV actress”; the actress may have left the shop or retired. Consumers should not treat advertising as verified fact.

For the actress, the use of her name, photos and titles by a shop can involve portrait rights, reputation and contractual authorisation. If the shop implies that she provides certain services, that adds risk. The Tokyo Metropolitan Police warn that using an identifiable person’s photo without consent can infringe portrait rights. [Tokyo Metropolitan Police: name and photo used without permission](https://www.keishicho.metro.tokyo.lg.jp/sodan/nettrouble/jirei/syozoken.html) There is no standard authorisation template or verification system for “AV actress on the roster” advertising in the public record.

For the industry, this is brand lending: the shop borrows the title, the actress borrows the customer base. The split of revenue and the bearing of risk are often unequal; the actress may receive a one-off fee while carrying long-term privacy and reputational risk.

## Risks: law, health, privacy and control

**Law**. Referral or recruitment may fall under the Employment Security Act; sexual services may fall under the Anti-Prostitution Act; unlicensed or out-of-scope operation may fall under the Entertainment Businesses Act; an AV contract may be breached. Liability depends on the act, the contract and the evidence.

**Health**. STI testing and condom use in AV and fūzoku differ by industry, shop and individual; one side’s testing cannot be assumed to cover the other. Japan’s Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare provides information on STI testing and consultation services, but there is no single testing standard across the two industries. [Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare: STIs](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/seisakunitsuite/bunya/kenkou_iryou/kenkou/kekkaku-kansenshou/seikansenshou/index.html)

**Privacy**. An AV stage name and a fūzoku stage name can be cross-identified; photos, shops and titles can be linked; family, fans and employers may find out; stalking and harassment are risks.

**Control**. Agencies or shops may use referral fees, debt, shifts and “refuse and lose work” to control a performer. The 2016 case and the Nikkei report raised the suspicion of work against the person’s will, but individual cases are for the courts. Project-based freelancers are especially vulnerable: they have no stable salary, paid leave or company benefits, and support after a problem is limited.

## Media, fans and the “go and meet her” imagination

AV actresses and fūzoku women are both becoming “idol-like”: AV actresses do events, gravure and social media; fūzoku women write photo diaries and manage bookings. The boundary is blurred in media. The Nikkan SOD article called this a “similar but different” idolisation.

Fans’ desire to “meet” is real: they buy titles, attend events and visit shops. But “the person seen in a title” and “the person providing a service in a shop” are not the same role; on-screen performance cannot be treated as real consent or a promise of service.

Media favour the headline “AV actress turns to fūzoku” because it offers a story of “fall” and “intimacy”; but it obscures income structure, contracts, recruitment and working conditions. For readers, the important thing is not to decide whether someone is “an AV actress” or “a fūzoku woman”, but to avoid treating the two as the same person and using a title as pressure.

## Common misconceptions

- **“An AV actress is a fūzoku woman.”** They are different occupations and legal frameworks; some people do both, but they are not the same job.
- **“Every fūzoku shop offers full service.”** Service ranges differ by business type; shop advertising is not proof of lawful service, and advertising cannot be the standard.
- **“All AV actresses work in fūzoku.”** There is no representative data; public cases are selective and no rate can be calculated.
- **“All fūzoku women are coerced.”** Some choose the work, some are controlled; it cannot be generalised, and no one should decide for the person.
- **“An AV title always raises income.”** It depends on fame, the shop and customer traffic; it is not a guarantee.
- **“A roster listing is always true.”** It may be old, unauthorised or invented; verification is needed.
- **“Side work is only a personal choice.”** Contracts, tax, health, privacy and recruitment law all matter; it is not purely personal.

## What the sources show

What this article can confirm: AV and fūzoku are legally different businesses; public cases show routes from fūzoku to AV, from AV to fūzoku and simultaneous work; the 2016 case shows that an agency referring AV actresses to a fūzoku shop can fall under the Employment Security Act, and police suspected work against the person’s will; an AV title can command a premium in the fūzoku market; AV income instability encourages side work; shop advertising may use names and photos without authorisation; the AV new law does not regulate fūzoku; there are no unified rules on side work, testing or authorisation across the two industries.

What it cannot confirm: the share of AV actresses who work in fūzoku, the share of fūzoku workers who move to AV, average incomes in either industry, the share of agencies involved in referrals, the share of shops using AV names, STI rates, the incidence of coercion, or the causal effect of side work on career length. Police cases, self-accounts, shop advertising and media selection all carry selection bias; without a denominator, “the cases we see” cannot be turned into “the rate for the whole”.

As of the source-check date, **17 November 2026**, no representative survey of AV actresses’ fūzoku work was found. Any general claim that “this is how AV actresses are” therefore goes beyond what the available public sources can support.
