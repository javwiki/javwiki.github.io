# AV actresses and contraception

In AV titles, “raw” and “creampie” are genre labels, not a record of the contraception used on set. Confusing on-screen performance with practice overstates how realistic the footage is and understates the pregnancy and infection risks performers face. This article reviews industry practice, regulation and the limits of the public record; it is not medical advice.

## Contraception and STI prevention are different problems

Contraception usually means preventing pregnancy; STI prevention means blocking transmission of pathogens. The two overlap but are not the same. Hormonal contraceptives greatly reduce the chance of pregnancy but do not stop HIV, syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and other infections; condoms are the only contraceptive method that reduces both pregnancy and several STIs. [WHO: Contraception methods](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/family-planning-contraception)

So “the actress is on the pill” does not mean “the shoot is safe.” Any account of on-set arrangements should separately establish whether condoms were used, whether hormonal contraception was used, whether STI tests were done, which tests they covered, and how long had passed since the last exposure.

## Why condomless scenes exist

“Raw,” “creampie” and “massive ejaculation” have long been selling points. By the mid-2010s, Japanese media were reporting a rise in condomless shooting as an industry problem, and studios, agencies and performers did not describe the rules in the same way. [Asahi Shimbun withnews: HIV infection and condom use](https://withnews.jp/article/f0181025003qq000000000000000W05s10101qq000018209A)

Former actress [Kaho Shibuya](../人物/女优/さ/し/澁谷果歩.md) said in a 2020 interview that creampie scenes pay more because the physical burden and risk are greater; that shoots include STI testing and actresses take low-dose pills; and that in recent years most creampie shots use a prepared “simulated semen.” She also stressed that these are risky measures and that viewers should not imitate them. [ViVi: Kaho Shibuya on contraception and pregnancy risk](https://www.vivi.tv/post165786/)

That testimony has two limits. It is one performer's experience, not an industry-wide statistic, and “mostly simulated” does not mean “no bodily fluid contact”: oral, vaginal and anal sex can still transmit infections.

| Method | Reduces pregnancy risk | Reduces STI risk | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Condoms | Yes | Yes (multiple pathogens) | Must be used correctly throughout; use on set varies by genre and negotiation |
| Low-dose oral contraceptive | Yes | No | Prescription medicine with contraindications and side effects |
| Emergency contraception | Yes (after the fact) | No | More effective the sooner it is taken; not routine contraception |
| Withdrawal, rhythm method | Limited | No | Not a reliable safeguard |

## Condoms and the economics of “raw” titles

A 2018 Asahi Shimbun report said condom use had risen at major studios' shoots but that some actual-intercourse (“honban”) scenes still went without. One agency figure said asking a studio to ban honban would lead to being dropped or paid less, so agencies could not make the demand on their own. [Asahi Shimbun withnews: conditions on set](https://withnews.jp/article/f0181025003qq000000000000000W05s10101qq000018209A)

Condomless sex is therefore not simply a personal preference; it is bound up with genre demand, studio requirements and a performer's bargaining power. Whether newcomers, independent performers and those marketed as “raw” have the same room to refuse needs to be established case by case. Whether “she agreed” was informed consent depends on whether she could refuse, whether refusal would cost her work, and whether she understood the infection risk.

## Low-dose pills and emergency contraception

Low-dose oral contraceptives (OCs) are prescription medicines in Japan and require a doctor's assessment. Thrombotic disorders and other conditions are contraindications, and smoking can affect suitability. OCs do not prevent STIs. [Japan Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology: OC guidance](https://www.jsog.or.jp/news/pdf/CQ30-31.pdf)

Industry recruitment and job descriptions sometimes present “taking a low-dose pill before the shoot” as a precondition for condomless work, and some say the agency or studio pays for it. These are statements by recruiters and industry sites; there is no single rule or public verification. [Another Pro: contraception on AV shoots](https://anotherpro.jp/columns/93/)

Emergency contraception is a post-coital backup, not a substitute for ongoing contraception, and it does not prevent STIs. Since 2 February 2026, pharmacies that meet certain requirements in Japan have been able to sell it without a prescription as a pharmacist-advised medicine, with a pharmacist's explanation at purchase; it is also available by prescription after in-person or online consultation. [MHLW: dispensing and sale of emergency contraception](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/kinnkyuuhininnyaku.html)

## What STI testing can and cannot guarantee

The AV industry has no single statutory testing regime. In a 2017 conversation, male performer [Shimiken](../人物/男优/清水健.md) (Ken Shimizu) said he was tested every two weeks, while actresses had “no rules on testing”; later, the policy changed to requiring a test result before shooting. Throat gonorrhoea and chlamydia were said to be common in the industry. [Nikkei Medical: a male performer and an infectious disease specialist](https://medical.nikkeibp.co.jp/leaf/all/cadetto/tuusin/201706/551486_2.html)

The AV Human Rights and Ethics Organization, a third-party body founded in 2017, handles industry self-regulation and consultation. In September 2018, an actress's HIV infection was found through performer testing, and the organization notified those concerned in October. Reporting said the infection did not occur during a shoot and that no spread to contacts was identified. [Asahi Shimbun withnews: HIV disclosure](https://withnews.jp/article/f0181025003qq000000000000000W05s10101qq000018209A)

Testing matters, but a negative result is not zero risk. HIV and other infections have a window period during which tests may be negative; the period varies by pathogen and test. [MHLW: HIV testing basics](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/hivkensa/knowledge/mame) Testing frequency, the panel of tests and who sees the results are settled among agencies, studios and performers; the public record contains no industry-wide standard.

## Law and self-regulation

The AV performer protection and relief law (the “AV new law”), in force in 2022, prohibits forcing sexual acts during filming. Before an appearance contract is concluded, it requires an explanation of matters including “the status of STI testing of the other party in the sexual act to be filmed.” [Cabinet Office: AV law explainer](https://www.gender.go.jp/policy/no_violence/avjk/pdf/seiritsu_kaisetsu.pdf), [full text of the law](https://laws.e-gov.go.jp/law/504AC1000000078)

The law thus makes “whether the partner has been tested” a pre-contract disclosure item, but it does not require condom use, mandate contraception or set a uniform testing frequency. Contraception and infection control still depend on industry self-regulation, individual contracts and on-set negotiation.

## When contraception becomes a means of control

Contraception can also be used to control performers. In a 2017 Asahi Shimbun report, an agency president gave women in their late teens and around twenty unapproved pills imported privately, saying they would “improve skin” and “enlarge breasts.” Some contracted chlamydia, gonorrhoea and candidiasis after condomless shoots, and a support group said performers were assigned to shoots before they had fully recovered. The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare said transferring unapproved drugs violates the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Act. [Asahi Shimbun withnews: coerced AV filming and pills](https://withnews.jp/article/f0171209000qq000000000000000W05s10101qq000015734A)

The issue is not simply whether she took a pill, but who decided, who supplied it, whether side effects were explained, and whether she could refuse the shoot. Treating the pill as a licence for condomless sex hides both the infection risk and the question of consent.

## Pregnancy, leave and work arrangements

AV work is usually contracted project by project, and the relationship between performer and studio is often handled as a freelance commission. Whether someone counts as a “worker” under the Labour Standards Act is judged on the substance of the relationship — including how work is directed — not on the contract's title. Employee protections such as maternity leave and childcare leave do not apply automatically just because the work is in AV. [MHLW: freelance transactions and working environment](https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/seisakunitsuite/bunya/koyou_roudou/koyoukintou/zaitaku/index_00002.html)

Pregnancy usually means pausing shoots, events and some commercial work, and the details depend on the individual contract and agency. The public record contains no industry-wide rules on leave, compensation or return, and no statistics on pregnancy rates. A sudden stop in an actress's updates is therefore not evidence that she is pregnant or retired.

## Common misconceptions

- **“A creampie is real ejaculation.”** It is a screen convention, and some scenes use simulated semen. The proportion and method vary by studio, era and scene, so no single description fits all.
- **“Taking the pill prevents STIs.”** Neither OCs nor emergency contraception prevent STIs.
- **“A negative test means no risk.”** Infections acquired within the window period may not be detected, and a test panel may not cover every pathogen.
- **“AV sets always require condoms.”** Practice varies by studio, genre and negotiation, and there is no uniform legal requirement.
- **“AV can serve as sex education.”** It is made for performance and sales, not as medical information on contraception or infection control.

## What the record shows

The record supports this much: Japan produces titles marketed as “raw” and “creampie”; condom use is not mandatory in every scene; some performers use prescription contraception and the industry treats STI testing as a self-regulatory tool; and the AV new law requires disclosure of the partner's testing status but imposes no contraception duty. Reporting also documents individual cases of condomless shoots, unapproved drug distribution and shooting before treatment was complete.

It does not establish industry-wide rates of condom use, pill use, STI incidence or pregnancy, nor how those differ by studio, performer tier or period. Clinic data, performer interviews and agency statements can carry selection bias or conflicts of interest, and statutory text does not prove how faithfully rules are followed.

As of **15 November 2026**, the date of this source check, no representative industry-wide survey was found. Any general claim that “AV actresses contracept in such-and-such a way” therefore goes beyond what the available public record can support.
